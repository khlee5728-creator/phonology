Exercise 1. Triple Codas and the Sonority Sequencing Principle (SSP)

In Section 6.5.6, several patterns for non-suffixed triple codas are discussed. Which of these (if any) violate(s) the Sonority Sequencing Principle? State the example(s) and your rationale.
Answer

The Sonority Sequencing Principle (SSP) requires that sonority decreases from the nucleus outward. In a coda, segments should decrease in sonority as we move away from the nucleus (nucleus > first coda segment > second > third).

Sonority scale (high to low): vowels > glides > liquids > nasals > fricatives > stops

English triple codas include patterns such as:

PatternExampleSSP statusRationale
nasal + fricative + stop: /nst/againstViolates SSPFricative [s] is more sonorous than stop [t] — OK so far; but nasal [n] before fricative [s] — nasal > fricative in sonority: valid decrease. Actually /nst/ does decrease. OK.
nasal + stop + stop: /nkt/instinctMarginal[n] (nasal) > [k] (stop) > [t] (stop) — two stops of equal sonority at end; SSP requires strict decrease, so this is a minor violation
liquid + fricative + stop: /lft/twelfthOK[l] > [f] > [t]: liquid > fricative > stop — proper decrease
fricative + stop + stop: /skt/askedMarginal violation[s] > [k] > [t]: fricative > stop > stop — two stops at end violate strict SSP
fricative + fricative + stop: /fθ/+stoptwelfths /fθs/Violates SSPTwo fricatives followed by [s] — no consistent decrease; this is the most problematic pattern

Conclusion: Patterns ending in two stops of equal sonority (like /nkt/, /skt/) marginally violate the SSP. The textbook treats these as tolerated exceptions in English's complex coda system. The /nst/ pattern (as in against) does not violate SSP if we accept that fricatives are more sonorous than stops.

Exercise 2. Ambisyllabicity

Which of the following would qualify for ambisyllabicity? Circle the word(s), state your rationale, and give the tree diagrams. metric, regime, anecdote, integrity, panic, majesty, Africa, rival, pity, study, radical, legal, action, many, liquid, penalty, garbage, picnic, spinach
Answer

Ambisyllabicity occurs when an intervocalic consonant in a two-syllable word belongs simultaneously to both the coda of the first syllable and the onset of the second syllable. Conditions: (1) the word has primary stress on the first syllable, (2) the first vowel is lax (short), (3) a single consonant intervenes between the two vowels.

regimeNo[ɹ̣ɪdʒim] — stress on second syllable; first vowel reduced
anecdoteNoThree syllables; /n/ after unstressed [æ] — complex structure

Exercise 3. Ambisyllabicity from Deletable Schwas

Some of the following words can create ambisyllabic consonants due to deletable schwas in fast speech. Circle the ones that are applicable and give the tree diagrams. broccoli, carpenter, separate, reference, minaret, cabinet, finally, miracle, veteran
Answer

Rule: When an unstressed schwa is deleted in fast speech, a consonant that was the onset of the schwa syllable may become the coda of the preceding syllable and simultaneously the onset of the following, creating ambisyllabicity.

WordCarefulFast speech (schwa deleted)Ambisyllabic C
broccoli[bɹ̣ɑkəli][bɹ̣ɑkli]Yes — /k/ becomes ambisyllabic between [ɑ] and [l]
carpenter[kɑɹ̣pəntɚ][kɑɹ̣pntɚ]Yes — /p/ or /n/ may become ambisyllabic with schwa deletion
separate (v)[sεpəɹ̣et][sεpɹ̣et]Yes — /p/ becomes ambisyllabic after schwa deletion
reference[ɹ̣εfəɹ̣əns][ɹ̣εfɹ̣əns]Yes — /f/ becomes ambisyllabic
minaret[mɪnəɹ̣εt][mɪnɹ̣εt]Yes — /n/ becomes ambisyllabic
cabinet[kæbɪnɪt][kæbnɪt]Yes — /b/ becomes ambisyllabic with medial vowel deletion
finally[faɪnəli][faɪnli]Yes — /n/ becomes ambisyllabic
miracle[mɪɹ̣ɪkəl][mɪɹ̣ɪkl]Yes — /k/ becomes ambisyllabic before syllabic /l/
veteran[vεtəɹ̣ən][vεtɹ̣ən]Yes — /t/ becomes ambisyllabic

All words in the list qualify. Schwa deletion in fast speech promotes consonants to ambisyllabic status whenever they fall between a stressed vowel and another syllable.

Exercise 4. Short vs. Long Vowel + Consonant Cluster Constraint

Consider the contrasts: Short V + CC: pimp, lint, sink (possible) Long V/diph. + C: wipe, light, bike, weep, seed, beak (possible) Long V/diph. + CC: mind, grind (possible) BUT *[maɪmb], *[maɪŋɡ], *[ɡɹ̣aɪmb], *[ɡɹ̣aɪŋɡ] (impossible) State the generalization.
Answer

Generalization: The Bimoraic Constraint / Weight Restriction on Rhymes

English syllable rhymes are maximally bimoraic (two moras). The following weight assignments apply:

  • Short vowel (lax) = 1 mora; long vowel or diphthong = 2 moras
  • A coda consonant adds 1 mora
PatternExampleMorasStatus
Short V + CCpimp [pɪmp]1 (V) + 1 (C) + 1 (C) = 3? But CC is counted as one position in some analyses — OKPermitted
Long V/diph. + Cwipe [waɪp]2 (diph.) + 1 (C) = 3 — still within limitsPermitted
Long V/diph. + CC (nasal+stop same place)mind [maɪnd]Permitted with specific cluster types (coronal)Permitted (coronal clusters allowed)
Long V/diph. + CC (other place)*[maɪmb], *[maɪŋɡ]Would be too heavy AND violate place co-occurrence restrictionsForbidden

The specific generalization: After a long vowel or diphthong, final consonant clusters are only permitted when the two consonants share the same place of articulation AND form a nasal + stop sequence. Specifically: /nd/, /ŋk/ after long vowels are permitted because the nasal is homorganic with the stop. But clusters of mixed place (labial+velar: *[mb], *[ŋɡ] after diphthongs) violate the constraint. The rule is: Long vowel/diphthong + CC final cluster → only homorganic nasal-stop clusters permitted.

Exercise 6. Final Cluster Simplification

English final consonant clusters are simplified by deleting the final member in certain contexts (e.g., /nd/ in "sand piles" [sæn paɪlz], /st/ in "first class" [fɚs klæs]), but not in others (e.g., /nd/ in "canned vegetables" [kænd vε…], /st/ in "missed goals" [mɪst ɡolz]). Analyze the following and state the generalization. hand-held, gift horse, wind-mill, left hand, hand rail, soft landing, grandfather, gift-wrap
Answer

Rule: Final cluster simplification (deletion of final C) occurs when the following word begins with a consonant of the same or similar manner/place — specifically, when the deleted consonant would form a homorganic geminate-like sequence or when the following consonant is an obstruent. It does NOT occur when the following word begins with a vowel or a consonant of distinctly different manner.

PhraseClusterDeletion possible?ResultRule
hand-held/nd/ + /h/Yes[hæn hεld]/d/ deleted before /h/ (obstruent follows)
gift horse/ft/ + /h/Yes[ɡɪf hɔɹ̣s]/t/ deleted before /h/
wind-mill/nd/ + /m/Yes[wɪn mɪl]/d/ deleted before /m/ (obstruent follows)
left hand/ft/ + /h/Yes[lεf hænd]/t/ deleted before /h/
hand rail/nd/ + /ɹ̣/No / marginal[hænd ɹ̣el]/ɹ̣/ is a sonorant — deletion less likely before sonorants
soft landing/ft/ + /l/No / marginal[sɔft lændɪŋ]/l/ is a sonorant — deletion blocked
grandfather/nd/ + /f/Yes[ɡɹ̣æn fɑðɚ]/d/ deleted before /f/ (obstruent)
gift-wrap/ft/ + /ɹ̣/No[ɡɪft ɹ̣æp]/ɹ̣/ is a sonorant — deletion blocked

Generalization: Final cluster simplification (deletion of the final obstruent) applies when the following word begins with an obstruent consonant. It does NOT apply when the following word begins with a sonorant consonant (nasal, liquid, glide) or a vowel. This is consistent with the broader principle that deletion is motivated by consonant cluster reduction across word boundaries, facilitated by an obstruent context.
