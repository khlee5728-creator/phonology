Exercise 1. Schwa Deletion in Fast Speech

In the following, schwa deletion occurs in words (a)–(k) in fast speech; the same is NOT possible in words (l)–(v). State the generalization. Pay special attention to morphologically related words such as (f)/(s), (g)/(v), (h)/(u), (i)/(t), (j)/(q), (k)/(r). Schwa deletable: (a) camera [kæmɹ̣ə], (b) veteran [vεtɹ̣ən], (c) aspirin [æspɹ̣ən], (d) temperature [tεmpɹ̣ətʃɚ], (e) reasonable [ɹ̣iznəbl̩], (f) imaginative [ɪmædʒnətɪv], (g) principal [pɹ̣ɪnspəl], (h) management [mændʒmənt], (i) testament [tεstmənt], (j) general [dʒεnɹ̣əl], (k) opera [ɑpɹ̣ə] Schwa NOT deletable: (l) famous, (m) vegetarian, (n) motivate, (o) pathology, (p) facilitate, (q) generality, (r) operatic, (s) imagination, (t) testimony, (u) managerial, (v) principality
Answer

Generalization:

Schwa deletion is possible when:

  1. The schwa is in a non-initial, non-final syllable that is unstressed AND not adjacent to the primary stressed syllable in a way that would create an impermissible consonant cluster after deletion.
  2. After deletion, the resulting consonant cluster must be a permissible onset or coda in English, OR must create an ambisyllabic consonant between existing syllables.
  3. The schwa's deletion must not result in the loss of a phonological contrast or morphological boundary that would affect meaning.

Schwa deletion is NOT possible when:

  • The schwa is adjacent to (before or after) the primary stressed syllable — deleting it would violate the foot structure (e.g., famous [fém-əs]: schwa is in the final syllable right after the stressed foot; motivate [mót-ə-vet]: schwa carries nuclear position).
  • The deletion would create a phonetically impermissible consonant cluster that does not exist in English onset/coda structure.
  • The morphologically related form (q)/(r)/(s) etc. bears stress on the syllable in question — schwa deletion is blocked when the same vowel position bears primary stress in any related form. This is the morphological blocking condition: compare general (deletion OK) vs. generality (deletion blocked because /ε/ is stressed in generality, preserving the full vowel quality in general as well in careful speech).

Exercise 3. Noun and Verb Stress Rules

Which of the following follow the main stress rules for nouns and verbs? State the generalizations and explain the ones that do not follow the rules. tobacco, reject, telephone, recognize, afternoon, include, pedigree, withhold, analysis, realize, persuade, cashier, movement
Answer

Main rules:

  • Disyllabic nouns: stress on penult (first syllable) — default
  • Disyllabic verbs: stress on final syllable (ult) — default
  • Trisyllabic+ nouns: stress penult if heavy (branching rhyme); else antepenult

Exercise 4. Tonic Accent Placement (Neutral Reading)

Determine where the tonic accent will be in the following (neutral, non-contrastive readings). (a) A: Are you coming to the movie? / B: I have exams to grade. (b) The dog barked. (c) The building's falling down. (d) I go to Boston, usually.
Answer

Principle: In neutral (non-contrastive) readings, the tonic accent (nuclear accent) falls on the last lexical item in the intonation unit — typically the final content word (noun, verb, adjective, adverb). This is the "default" or "broad focus" pattern.

#UtteranceTonic accent onExplanation
(a)B: I have exams to GRADE.gradeFinal lexical verb; B is responding with new information — "grade" is the last new content word
(b)The dog BARKED.barkedFinal predicate (verb); in a simple declarative with no prior context, the verb receives the tonic accent
(c)The building's falling DOWN.downFinal element; "down" is a directional particle carrying the rhematic (new) information
(d)I go to BOSTON, usually.Boston"Boston" is the final content word (noun) — the nuclear accent lands there; "usually" is a disjunct/adverb that follows the tonic unit

Exercise 7. Extended Transcription Passage

Transcribe the passage about "English in America" (continued) from J. Jenkins, World Englishes (2002): "During the seventeenth century, English spread to southern parts of America and the Caribbean as a result of the slave trade..."
Answer

This is an extended open transcription exercise. Key stress and intonation features:

  • Stress patterns of key words: seventeenth [sεvntinθ] — final syllable stressed (compound ordinal); Caribbean [kærɪbiən] — variable stress (US: ka-ríb-be-an; UK: ca-rib-BE-an); Philadelphia [fɪlәdεlfiə] — 4th syllable stressed; independence [ɪndɪpεndəns] — 3rd syllable; Declaration [dεkləreʃən] — 3rd syllable
  • Function word reduction: as [əz], of [əv], the [ðə], a [ə], and [ənd], for [fɚ], were [wɚ], had [həd], who [hu], but [bət]
  • Flapping: seventeenth /t/ between vowels; initially, century, initially [ɪnɪʃəli], captors [kæptɚz→kæpɾɚz], Britain [bɹ̣ɪɾən]
  • Connected speech features: "slave trade" — /v/ before /t/ may assimilate; "mother tongues" — /ɚ/ + /t/ linked; "next generation" — /t/ before /dʒ/: coalescence possible [nεktʃdʒεnəreʃən→nεksdʒεnəreʃən]
