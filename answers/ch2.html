Exercise 1. Circle the Correct Alternative(s)

(a) If two languages have the same sounds, then they (sometimes/always/often/never) have different phonologies. (b) If the phonetic difference between two sounds serves as the basis for distinguishing words, then the difference is (distinctive/phonemic/non-predictable/allophonic/predictable). (c) Occurrences of the allophones of a single phoneme are (always/sometimes/often/never) predictable. (d) Allophones of a single phoneme are (sometimes/often/always/never) phonetically similar. (e) If two phonetically similar sounds are in complementary distribution, then they are (sometimes/often/always/never) allophones of the same phoneme. (f) If two sounds are in free variation, then they are (sometimes/always/never) allophones of the same phoneme. (g) Speakers of a language tend to be (more/less/equally) consciously aware of phonemes than of allophones. (h) Two sounds that appear in a minimal pair (sometimes/always/never) belong to distinct phonemes. (i) If two sounds are not phonemically distinct, their distribution overlaps/does not overlap.
Answer
#Correct answerExplanation
(a)sometimesSame phonetic inventory can be organized into different phonological contrasts across languages.
(b)distinctive / phonemic / non-predictableA difference that distinguishes words is phonemic (contrastive) and non-predictable from environment.
(c)alwaysBy definition, allophones of a single phoneme are in complementary distribution — their occurrence is conditioned by (predictable from) the phonetic environment.
(d)alwaysAllophones of the same phoneme must be phonetically similar (a requirement for grouping them together).
(e)alwaysComplementary distribution + phonetic similarity = allophones of the same phoneme (the standard test).
(f)alwaysFree variation means two sounds alternate in the same environment without changing meaning — they are therefore allophones of the same phoneme.
(g)moreSpeakers are typically more aware of phonemic contrasts (which affect meaning) than of allophonic variation (which is largely sub-conscious).
(h)alwaysA minimal pair by definition differs in exactly one sound that creates a meaning distinction, proving those sounds are separate phonemes.
(i)does not overlapSounds that are not phonemically distinct are in complementary distribution — they never appear in the same environment.

Exercise 7. Distribution of [s] and [ʃ] in Child Speech (T, age 4;3)

Examine the distribution of [s] and [ʃ] in the speech of T (4;3), a child with phonological disorders, and determine whether their distribution is complementary or contrastive. State your evidence. sail [ʃel], pushy [pʊʃi], seek [ʃik], save [ʃev], Sam [ʃæm], gas [ɡæs], grass [ɡɹæ̣s], fasten [fæsәn], crash [kɹæ̣s], ship [ʃɪp], Irish [aɪɹɪ̣s], fashion [fæʃәn]
Answer

Distribution: Complementary

SoundEnvironmentExamples
[ʃ]Word-initial position; before tense/front vowelssail→[ʃel], seek→[ʃik], save→[ʃev], Sam→[ʃæm], ship→[ʃɪp]
[s]Word-medial and word-final positionsgas→[ɡæs], grass→[ɡɹæ̣s], fasten→[fæsәn], crash→[kɹæ̣s], Irish→[aɪɹɪ̣s]

Rationale: [ʃ] appears in word-initial position where adult English has /s/, while [s] appears medially and finally (where [ʃ] is the adult target in fashion, pushy, crash etc.). The two sounds never appear in the same environment — their distribution is complementary. They are allophones of a single underlying phoneme in T's system, with [ʃ] in word-initial position and [s] elsewhere.

Exercise 8. Distribution of /ɹ̣/ realizations in Child Speech (B, age 4;1)

Examine the following data from B (4;1). The /ɹ̣/ targets show three different realizations: [ɹ̣], [w], or "zero" (deleted). What kind of distribution do these realizations reveal? rich [ɹɪ̣tʃ], raise [ɹẹz], red [ɹε̣d], more [mo], door [do], deer [di], wrong [ɹↄ̣ŋ], correct [kↄwεk], mirror [mɪwә], rain [ɹẹn], room [ɹụm], parrot [pæwәt], roller [ɹↄ̣lә], parade [pәwed], Henry [hεnɹị̣]
Answer

Distribution: Complementary

RealizationEnvironmentExamples
[ɹ̣] (target-like)Word-initial (onset) positionrich, raise, red, wrong, rain, room, roller
[w]Intervocalic (between vowels) / post-consonant medialcorrect→[kↄwεk], mirror→[mɪwә], parrot→[pæwәt], parade→[pәwed]
∅ (deleted)Word-final (coda) positionmore→[mo], door→[do], deer→[di]

Rationale: The three realizations are in complementary distribution — each appears in a distinct phonological environment. [ɹ̣] occurs in syllable-initial (onset) position; [w] occurs in intervocalic/medial positions; ∅ occurs in word-final (coda) position. Because the environments are mutually exclusive and the sounds are phonetically similar (all sonorant, approximant-like), they function as allophones of a single /ɹ̣/ phoneme in B's system.

Note: Henry [hεnɹị̣] — the word-final [ɹ̣] before a vowel in a name boundary may be treated differently; this is an edge case.

Exercise 9. Maasai Data — Phonemic Status of [t], [d], [ð]

(a) Examine Maasai data and determine whether [t], [d], and [ð] belong to one, two, or three phonemes. [ɓaða] "dangerous", [endorop] "bribe him", [tasat] "disabled", [tisila] "sift it", [taruɓini] "binoculars", [oltuli] "buttock", [iltoi] "barrel", [ɗalut] "mischievous", [endaraða] "fight each other", [indai] "'you' plural", [endulelei] "apple", [eŋɡiruðoðo] "fright", [emɓiðir] "female wart hog", [enɗaraða] "thunder" (b) Are their distributions in Maasai and English the same or different? (c) Who will have greater difficulty learning each other's language?
Answer

(a) In Maasai, [t], [d], and [ð] appear to be in complementary distribution and thus represent allophones of a single phoneme (or possibly two phonemes):

  • [t] appears in word-initial position and in certain consonant clusters
  • [d] appears after nasals (intervocalically following nasal consonants)
  • [ð] appears intervocalically (between vowels)

Since none of the three sounds appear in the same environment contrasting with each other (no minimal pairs found), they are allophones of one or two phonemes in Maasai.

(b) The distributions differ from English. In English, [t], [d], and [ð] are three separate phonemes (shown by minimal pairs: teem–deem, tin–thin, den–then). In Maasai, these same sounds are allophones — predictable variants — not phonemically distinct.

(c) The English speaker learning Maasai will have greater difficulty. English speakers perceive [t], [d], and [ð] as completely separate phonemes and will struggle to hear them as the "same" sound in Maasai. The Maasai speaker learning English must learn to contrast sounds they previously treated as variants — this is also difficult but involves learning new distinctions rather than collapsing existing ones.

Exercise 10. Hindi Data — Phonemic Status of [t], [tʰ], [d]

(a) Examine Hindi data and determine the phonemic status of [t], [tʰ], and [d]. [tantrik] "tantra", [tʰan] "a bolt of cloth", [dan] "donate", [batʰ] "words", [tal] "beat", [tʰal] "plate", [patʰak] "one who studies", [bad] "later", [dal] "lentil", [pʰatak] "a gate" (b) Are their distributions the same or different from English? (c) Who will have greater difficulty?
Answer

(a) In Hindi, [t], [tʰ], and [d] are three distinct phonemes. Evidence from minimal pairs:

  • [tal] "beat" vs. [tʰal] "plate" — minimal pair for /t/ vs. /tʰ/
  • [dan] "donate" vs. [tan] (part of [tantrik]) — /d/ vs. /t/ contrast
  • [dal] "lentil" vs. [tal] "beat" — /d/ vs. /t/ in initial position

Aspiration ([tʰ]) is phonemically distinctive in Hindi, while in English [t] and [tʰ] are allophones of the same phoneme /t/ (aspirated word-initially in stressed syllables, unaspirated after /s/).

(b) The distributions are different. English treats aspiration as allophonic (predictable); Hindi treats it as phonemic (distinctive). Both languages have /t/ and /d/ as separate phonemes, so that contrast is shared.

(c) The English speaker learning Hindi will have greater difficulty with the /t/ vs. /tʰ/ contrast, since English speakers perceive aspirated and unaspirated stops as the same phoneme and must learn to hear and produce a new contrast. The Hindi speaker has less difficulty with English's /t/ vs. /d/ (already a known contrast) but must suppress the aspiration distinction.

Exercise 11. German Data — [ç] and [x]

Examine German data and determine whether [ç] and [x] are allophones of the same phoneme or separate phonemes. [abmaxә] "to remove", [εçtә] "to ban", [axt] "eight", [ε:nliç] "resembling", [blε:çәn] "small blister", [drↄliç] "amusing", [εlç] "elk", [fraxt] "carriage", [fruxt] "fruit", [ɡlaɪç] "equal", [knoplaʊx] "garlic", [mεçtiç] "powerful", [ho:x] "high", [laxәn] "to laugh", [lox] "hole", [fεçtәn] "to fence"
Answer

Conclusion: [ç] and [x] are allophones of a single phoneme in German.

AllophoneEnvironmentExamples
[ç]After front vowels (/i, ε, ε:, e, aɪ/) and after consonants[εçtә], [ε:nliç], [blε:çәn], [drↄliç], [εlç], [mεçtiç], [ɡlaɪç], [fεçtәn]

The two sounds are phonetically similar (both are dorsal fricatives). Therefore, they are allophones of a single German phoneme /x/ (or /ç/ as the underlying form).

Exercise 12. Persian Data — [r], [r̥], and [ɾ]

Examine Persian data and determine the phonemic status of [r], [r̥], and [ɾ]. [aɾam] "calm", [aɾezu] "wish", [kæɾim] "giving", [ræhim] "giver", [ʃir̥] "lion", [pænir̥] "cheese", [ziɾe] "cumin", [zærd] "yellow", [farsi] "Persian", [musafir̥] "traveler", [kæbir̥] "grand", [bæɾe] "sheep", [nærm] "soft", [ræht] "laundry", [ræʃid] "strong", [moder̥] "mother", [sefer̥] "trip", [pæɾiveʃ] "angel looking"
Answer

Conclusion: [r], [r̥], and [ɾ] appear to be allophones of a single phoneme /r/ in Persian, in complementary distribution.

AllophoneEnvironmentExamples
[ɾ] (tap/flap)Intervocalic position (between vowels)aɾam, aɾezu, kæɾim, ziɾe, bæɾe, pæɾiveʃ
[r] (trill)Word-initial position and after consonantsræhim, ræht, ræʃid, nærm, zærd, farsi

Rationale: The three realizations appear in mutually exclusive environments and are phonetically similar (all rhotic). No minimal pairs contrast them. They are therefore allophones of a single Persian phoneme /r/, with distribution conditioned by position in the word.

Exercise 14. Extended Transcription Passage

Transcribe the passage about "the spread of English" (continued) from Trudgill & Hannah (2002): "It was not until the 17th century that the English language began the geographical and demographic expansion..."
Answer

This is an extended open transcription exercise. Key phonological points to apply:

  • Function word reduction: was [wəz], not [nɑt], that [ðət], the [ðə/ði], of [əv], and [ænd/ənd], in [ɪn], it [ɪt], with [wɪð]
